    <p class="section-kicker">Physics Research</p>
    <h1>Atmospheric Neutrino Simulation</h1>
    <p class="detail-subtitle">A compact deep-sea Cherenkov concept for GeV atmospheric-neutrino oscillations</p>
  </section>

  <section class="detail-section">
    <p>
      Large neutrino telescopes turn naturally transparent media into enormous particle detectors. IceCube in
      Antarctic ice, KM3NeT in the Mediterranean Sea, and Baikal-GVD in Lake Baikal instrument cubic-kilometer
      volumes with optical sensors that record Cherenkov light from neutrino-induced charged particles. These
      observatories are central to high-energy neutrino astronomy, while denser configurations such as DeepCore
      and ORCA extend the same detection principle toward the GeV energy range.
    </p>
    <p>
      Atmospheric neutrinos provide a natural beam for studying flavor oscillations. They arrive over a broad
      range of energies and travel distances ranging from tens of kilometers to the diameter of the Earth. The
      oscillation pattern is therefore organized by the ratio <span class="inline-math">\(L/E\)</span>, where
      <span class="inline-math">\(L\)</span> is inferred from the neutrino direction and
      <span class="inline-math">\(E\)</span> from its reconstructed energy. Measurements by DeepCore and ORCA
      have established that oscillation information can be recovered in natural media through the combined
      reconstruction of event topology, direction, and energy.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section class="detail-section">
    <h2>The Hai-Ling/TRIDENT Opportunity</h2>
    <p>
      Hai-Ling/TRIDENT is being developed in the South China Sea as a next-generation deep-sea neutrino
      telescope, with primary goals in high-energy astrophysical neutrino astronomy and all-flavor detection.
      Beyond its high-energy science program, the project is establishing a new experimental foundation for
      underwater neutrino detection in China: a characterized deep-sea site, measured optical properties,
      hybrid digital optical module technology, in-situ calibration strategies, and large-scale simulation and
      deployment experience.
    </p>
    <p>
      This platform creates an opportunity to investigate a complementary question at lower energy. The sparse,
      large-volume geometry favored for high-energy neutrino astronomy is not automatically optimal for GeV
      atmospheric neutrinos. Our study therefore takes the South China Sea environment and related detector
      technologies as motivation for a separate, GeV-oriented reference concept.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section class="detail-section">
    <h2>Why a More Compact Detector Array?</h2>
    <p>
      GeV neutrino interactions produce less Cherenkov light and more spatially compact event patterns than
      TeV--PeV events. Recovering the oscillation signal consequently requires denser optical sampling, strong
      event containment, and sufficient timing and charge information to distinguish track-like from
      cascade-like topologies. Direction reconstruction determines the propagation distance
      <span class="inline-math">\(L\)</span>, while energy reconstruction determines
      <span class="inline-math">\(E\)</span>; both must remain accurate enough for the oscillatory structure in
      reconstructed <span class="inline-math">\(L/E\)</span> to survive detector smearing.
    </p>
    <p><strong>The central question is:</strong></p>
    <p>
      Can a compact deep-sea Cherenkov array retain enough topology, direction, and energy information to support
      an atmospheric-neutrino oscillation measurement in the GeV range?
    </p>
    <ul class="clean-list">
      <li>Dense optical sampling improves photon statistics for low-energy events.</li>
      <li>Track/cascade identification defines oscillation-sensitive event samples.</li>
      <li>Zenith-angle reconstruction maps each event to an atmospheric-neutrino baseline.</li>
      <li>Energy and direction together determine the reconstructed \(L/E\) distribution.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>
